Iron Vale · identity exploration · option: modular assets

THE MODULAR
BORDER KIT

Panels stop being CSS and become drawn art. A hand-placed nine-slice — four corners, four rails, one fill, eight pixels square — means one panel can be riveted iron and the next carved oak, at no layout cost.

A box-shadow ring can only ever draw a rectangle of uniform thickness. That is precisely why every panel in Iron Vale looks like every other panel, and why the chrome reads machine-made: the machine had no other move available. A tile kit gives the corners somewhere to put a nail.

01 · the materials

Four sets, cut from one grammar

A material in this kit is eight colours and one corner stamp. The eight colours are a band profile read outer→inner: rim, chamfer, four face rows, inner shade, inner rim. Every rail and every corner is generated from that profile by a single rule — a pixel's colour is P[min(distance to each outer edge)]. That gives concentric bands, which is a frame; grain therefore always runs along the rail, the way a plank is actually cut. Two hand-placed things finish the corner: the mitre seam, one diagonal pixel run where the two rails meet, and a catch light on the chamfer's corner, because a chamfer catches more light at a corner than along a run. The ornament is then driven straight through the seam — which is exactly what a peg is for.

02 · the sheet

The sheet, drawn

Each set is a 24×24 sheet: nine 8×8 tiles laid out the way a nine-slice expects them. Shown at 10× with a pixel grid; the heavy lines are tile boundaries.

Iron, tile by tile

The reference set, broken out. Note that t and b are not the same tile and l and r are not the same tile: the chamfer stays on the outside and the shade stays in the well, so the frame reads as a real chamfered ring viewed straight on rather than as a lit object with a confused sun.

03 · the data

Ships in the project's own sprite format

Every tile below is expressed exactly as static/js/pixel.js expects it — a palette map plus row strings, '.' transparent. This is transcribable into SPRITES as-is; nothing new needs to be invented to hold it.

In practice you would not paste 36 tiles. The whole kit is 4 profiles, 4 corner stamps and 4 rail marks — about 40 hand-placed pixels per material — and the 36 tiles are generated from those at boot, the same way monsters are generated from DNA. 05 · the rule

  
Material has meaning

  
A kit that assigns materials at random is a skin. A kit that assigns them by what the place
  is is an identity — the player learns to read the frame before they read the words, and a
  panel arriving in iron is already a warning.

  

  
The one rule: material follows permanence and consequence,
  never screen or feature. The Undercroft's shop panel is canvas, because Pip pitches it and strikes it;
  the Undercroft's gate is stone, because it was there before Pip. Toasts and tooltips are always canvas
  — they are pitched for a moment and taken down — which is why a toast never needs to explain
  that it is temporary.







  
06 · the detail budget

  
Corners are where the money goes

  
A rail repeats, so a rail must stay quiet. A corner appears exactly once per panel per corner,
  so a corner can afford to be a specific object. Shown at 6×.

  


  
Ornaments that a repeating tile can never do

  
These are overlay sprites, positioned once, sitting above the rails. They are the reason the kit is
  a kit and not a texture: the wax ran down this panel's left rail, on this evening.

08 · the phone case

  
390 wide, where chrome costs real money

  
Two 16px rails eat 32 of 390. The answer is not to thin the kit — it is to notice that the rail
  is the padding. Today's .win spends 2px of border plus 14px of padding on each
  side. The kit spends 16px of rail plus 7px of gap. Net cost: 14px of the 390, and it buys the
  entire identity. Bleeding the panels to the screen edge on phone gives back 20 of those 14 and reads
  more like a game, not less.

  







  
09 · the mechanism

  
How nine tiles become a panel — and which CSS actually survives

  
The obvious answer is border-image. I tested it rather than trusting it, and the
  obvious answer is the wrong one. Measurements below are real: Chromium 141, tiles rendered, the page
  screenshotted, the screenshot's pixels read back and run-length counted across the top rail. A rail is
  exact when every source pixel became a run of identical length.

The surprise: DPR 2 saves repeat completely — the half-CSS-pixel
  centring offset lands on a whole device pixel, so every width is exact. That means border-image looks
  perfect on every modern phone and perfect in these very screenshots, and quietly wrong on a 1×
  desktop monitor at odd panel widths. That is the worst possible failure mode: invisible to whoever
  builds it.


  
Nine background layers, measured

  
background-repeat anchors its phase to the element's origin instead of centring it.
  Measured across the same widths: exact at DPR 1, 2 and 3, at every width tried, with no conditions.
  So the kit is built from nine background layers on one element — which is also, pleasingly, literal:
  the nine-slice is nine slices.

  

  
No extra DOM. No border at all — the rails are painted, and
  padding holds the content off them. Corner layers are listed first because in CSS the first
  background layer paints on top, so the corners cover the rails running underneath them. The
  6px 6px 0 cast shadow survives untouched; it just moves to 8px 8px so the whole
  panel, chrome and shadow, sits on one 8px grid.







  
10 · honestly

  
What this changes, keeps, and costs

  

    

      
What it changes

      
    
        
  • Panels become art. .win's three stacked box-shadow rings are deleted and
        replaced by nine background layers pointing at sprite data.
    • 
        
  • Corners carry objects. A rivet, a peg, a grommet, a chisel peck — things a
        border-width cannot express at any thickness.
    • 
        
  • The title stops being a cutout. .win-title's top:-14px
        notch over var(--bg) cannot survive a 16px rail. It becomes a plaque nailed to the
        rail: same span, same text, new rule.
    • 
        
  • Material becomes vocabulary. Four sets, assigned by permanence and consequence, learned
        in about three screens.
    • 
        
  • Chrome thickness doubles, 2px → 16px, and the panel starts to feel like an
        object with a front and a back.
    • 
      

    

    

      
What it keeps

      
    
        
  • Every button, chip, statbar, table and input is untouched. The bevel-key idiom is the
        best thing in the current stylesheet; the kit changes the room, not the furniture.
    • 
        
  • Square corners, no radius, no blur. The kit is more rectangular than what it replaces,
        not less.
    • 
        
  • The palette. Every colour here is from assets/palettes/town_palette.png
        or base.png, or is an existing token. The rim colour is literally
        --bg, so panels still float on the same void.
    • 
        
  • The cast shadow and the fixed vignette, unchanged.
    • 
        
  • The fonts at native pixel sizes, unchanged. quanta-strike-18 and
        -20 finally have a job: section headings.
    • 
        
  • The sprite pipeline. Tiles are {p,r} rows in pixel.js,
        rendered by the same canvas path as every monster.
    • 
      

    

    

      
What it costs

      
    
        
  • Authoring. Not 36 tiles — 4 profiles, 4 stamps, 4 rail marks. But every new
        material is a real drawing decision and there is no shortcut for the first pass on each.
    • 
        
  • border-image is out. Measured, not assumed: no repeat mode is pixel-exact across DPRs.
        The nine-layer background approach costs one long background-image declaration
        instead.
    • 
        
  • The rail phase does not meet the corner. When a panel's width is not a multiple of 16,
        the last rail tile is clipped where the corner starts. On stone this reads as a closer stone; on
        canvas as a cut hem. It is a reading, not a fix.
    • 
        
  • Asymmetric sides need a second layer. A torn bottom hem or a knocked-off corner cannot
        come from the nine-slice — those are positioned overlay sprites. Fine, but it is more DOM.
    • 
        
  • ~30 panel-ish classes in the stylesheet. They do not all convert. Proposed triage:
        .win and the raised surfaces take the kit at 2×; menus, toasts, bubbles and
        tooltips take it at 1×; rows, dividers, cards and chips keep the existing 2px bevel and are
        not touched at all. That is two new rules and an audit, not a rewrite — but the audit is real
        work and will surface three or four surfaces that are neither.
    • 
        
  • Below ~40px of content the ornament turns to mush. At 1× a rivet is 4 screen
        pixels wide. Handled by stopping rather than shrinking: under that, a surface is a chip, not a
        panel, and keeps the existing 2px bevel. Shown breaking, on purpose, in section 07.
    • 
      

    

  

  
One thing this option deliberately does not do: it does
  not touch layout, spacing rhythm, navigation, or a single line of copy. It is a chrome swap. That is
  either its great virtue or its limit, depending on how much of the “looks AI” problem lives
  in the chrome — and the honest answer is: some of it, not all of it.
